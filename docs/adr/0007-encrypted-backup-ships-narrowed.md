# ADR-0007 — The encrypted seed-QR backup ships, narrowed to one justification

- **Status**: accepted
- **Date**: 2026-08-15
- **Decides**: [#7 — Does the encrypted seed-QR backup earn its place in v1?](https://github.com/allisson/aobs/issues/7)
- **Construction**: [#6 — Argon2id parameters and AEAD](https://github.com/allisson/aobs/issues/6),
  `docs/research/06-backup-crypto.md`

## Context

The feature exports key material from a device whose entire premise is that key material never leaves
in a recoverable form. It was challenged rather than rubber-stamped.

**The strongest argument against was not the two-artifact objection.** It is that **the feature aobs
was copying is not the feature Coldcard and Passport ship.** Their backups carry device *settings* —
multisig registrations, derivation config, labels, PIN state — which is what makes them worth having
and expensive to reconstruct. aobs is amnesic and single-sig, so its backup can contain nothing but
the seed: it is strictly a re-encoding of the mnemonic, not a superset.

The survival objection is quantifiable: two required artifacts give (1−p)², **strictly worse than one
mnemonic on steel** — but it bites only if the QR *replaces* the mnemonic backup rather than
supplementing it. For passphrase users it is a *third* artifact, not a second.

The prior art also converges on one criticism: encryption does not remove a single point of failure,
it **moves it from the physical medium to the key**, converting a loud theft (someone took the
object) into a silent one (someone photographed it) with unlimited offline brute force.

## Decision

**It ships, as a *secondary* backup whose single justification is storage the user does not fully
trust** — a cloud drive, a semi-trusted safe deposit box, a phone photo. Steel cannot serve that
scenario; that is the whole of what encryption buys here. The value was never the content, it was
that ciphertext can be stored where plaintext cannot.

**The key is generated by aobs and never typed by the user:** 8 words from the EFF long list,
independently selected with replacement — 103.398 bits. Under Argon2id (m = 64 MiB, t = 3, p = 4)
that is **127.02 effective bits**, and **8 is the smallest word count that reaches the 128-bit floor
once the KDF is counted**. Seven lands at 114.10, a real 16,000× reduction. There is no room to drop
to 7 for usability.

**The envelope carries nothing identifying the wallet** — no fingerprint, no descriptor, no filename
hint. Krux salts its KDF with the wallet fingerprint *in cleartext*, so a photographed QR tells the
finder which wallet it is before they spend a cycle attacking it.

## Consequences

1. **Secondary by construction.** The QR is offered only after the mnemonic is confirmed recorded —
   structural, not advisory. In practice the door *is* the gate, because a created wallet arrived via
   a full 24-word retype and an imported one by typing a mnemonic the user holds.
2. **Type-back required.** The QR displays only after the user re-enters the 8 generated words
   correctly, from their own transcription, with the words off screen. The password is unmemorable,
   so mis-transcription is the top loss mode and it surfaces years later at restore.
3. **Passphrase users are offered the backup, not refused.** The passphrase stays out of it, so
   refusing buys no security. **The passphrase-in-use bit in the AAD is what makes this safe** —
   without it, a passphrase user restoring gets a valid, correctly decrypted, completely empty wallet
   with no error anywhere, and Poly1305 cannot catch it.
4. **The 8 words are as critical as the mnemonic, and the UI says so in those terms** — same
   typography, and one sentence that is the security model: *store this QR and the 8 words in
   different places; either one alone is useless, both together are your wallet.*
5. **The 8 words are never on screen at the same time as the QR.** Ciphertext and its password in one
   camera frame make a single photograph total, silent compromise.
6. **Parameters are fixed by the version byte, not stored in the file.** Nobody can hand us a file
   claiming t=1, and there is no resource-exhaustion knob. `age` and Krux both carry the work factor
   in the envelope; `rage` ships `--max-work-factor` precisely to bound it.
7. **Add-only version registry: 0x01 is never desupported**, on a device with no update mechanism.
   Krux carries four cipher modes across ~12 versions as the precedent for where that ends up.

**Accepted costs:** the EFF wordlist in the ISO (~60 KB), an **unaudited `argon2` crate inside the
98% bar**, a bespoke container, a restore path, and a permanent versioning commitment.

## Alternatives rejected

- **Cut the feature** — leaves no answer for untrusted storage, which is the one scenario steel
  cannot serve.
- **SLIP-39** — m-of-n split with no password to lose, and a reviewed spec; rejected as a bigger
  build and **unauthenticated by design** ("there is no way to verify that the correct passphrase was
  used"), trading away the loud-failure property this construction is built around.
- **Let the user choose the password** (Krux) — a character-class meter scores `Password1!` as
  "Strong", and Krux's own docs concede a weak key means "NO protection".
- **Reuse `age`** — wrong KDF (scrypt), attacker-controlled work factor, no slot for our associated
  data, ~200+ bytes of header for a 32-byte secret, and no published audit for the Rust crate. What
  we reuse from `age` is its *judgement*: ChaCha20-Poly1305 and the all-zero-nonce argument.
- **Use BIP39 words as the backup password** — 10 words gives 110 bits and the list is already on the
  device, but **a device that shows two different sets of BIP39 words will eventually have someone
  restore the wrong list**. The EFF words' lexical distinctness *is* the safety feature.
- **Raise Argon2id to 1 GiB** — +4.41 bits for a 16× larger allocation on a RAM-resident system;
  one extra EFF word buys 12.92 bits for free.
